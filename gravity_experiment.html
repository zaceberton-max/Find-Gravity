Aim & Hypothesis

Why does a cart speed up on a ramp?

When a cart is released on a frictionless incline, the component of gravity acting parallel to the ramp, g sinθ, accelerates it downward. This investigation measures that acceleration at five ramp angles in Melbourne, to find an experimental value for g.

Experimental Model

Ramp, cart & release point

The wooden plank was fixed at length L = 1.60 m and raised to five different heights using wooden blocks. The cart was released from rest at the top each time and timed over the full length of the ramp.

sinθ = 0.07
Cart released from rest · sinθ cycling through all five trial heights
Method

How each trial was run

  1. The ramp was set to five different heights: 0.105 m, 0.165 m, 0.224 m, 0.280 m and 0.378 m, keeping ramp length constant at 1.60 m.
  2. The cart was released from rest and timed over the full 1.60 m distance.
  3. Three trials were completed at each height, and an average time calculated.
  4. Acceleration was calculated using a = 2s / t².
  5. sinθ was calculated using h / L.
  6. Acceleration was plotted against sinθ, with the gradient representing experimental g.

Acceleration Analysis

a ∝ sinθ

Plotting acceleration against sinθ produced a strong, positive linear relationship. The gradient of the line of best fit gives the experimental value of g.

a = 9.08·sinθ + 0.0028  ·  R² ≈ 0.997
Conclusion

A reasonable estimate of g

The investigation determined the gravitational acceleration in Melbourne by measuring the acceleration of a cart on an inclined ramp at five angles. The results supported the hypothesis: a strong positive linear relationship between acceleration and sinθ, consistent with a = g sinθ.

Main limitation: human reaction time on the stopwatch, which is squared in a = 2s/t² so small timing errors are amplified. Friction between cart and ramp likely also reduced the measured acceleration slightly below the accepted value. Reliability could be improved with more trials and electronic timing gates.
